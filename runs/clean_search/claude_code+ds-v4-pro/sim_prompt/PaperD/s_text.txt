**LLMs for Automated Scientific Research.** Recent work has explored the use of large language models to automate various stages of the scientific research process. Lu et al. [1] introduced the AI Scientist, an end-to-end framework that uses frontier LLMs to generate research ideas, design and execute experiments via code generation, produce scientific papers, and perform automated peer review—all at low computational cost. This prompt-driven pipeline demonstrated the feasibility of fully automated open-ended scientific discovery, though it relies on proprietary commercial models and lacks a trainable self-improvement mechanism. Ifargan et al. [2] proposed data-to-paper, a multi-agent LLM system that autonomously conducts hypothesis-driven research from raw data to human-verifiable manuscripts, with programmatic back-tracing for transparency. Si et al. [3] conducted a large-scale human evaluation study showing that LLM-generated research ideas were judged as more novel than human-generated ones by over 100 NLP experts, while also identifying limitations in idea diversity and feasibility. These prior systems, however, predominantly rely on API-based commercial LLMs and lack mechanisms for iterative self-improvement through reinforcement learning, which our work addresses by post-training open-source models within a closed feedback loop.

**Automated Peer Review with Large Language Models.** The use of LLMs for evaluating scientific papers has attracted considerable research attention. Chu et al. [4] proposed PRE, a peer-review-based framework that uses multiple LLMs as reviewers to evaluate candidate model outputs, employing a qualification mechanism to select reliable reviewer models and produce final assessments. Tyser et al. [5] developed AI-driven review systems that evaluate LLM alignment with human reviews through pairwise preference comparisons and automated preference prediction, introducing systems for automated review generation and reviewer bias analysis. Beyond these prompt-based approaches, existing work has also identified significant challenges: LLM-generated reviews often exhibit overconfidence, systematic score overestimation, and weak correlation with human judgments, highlighting the gap between general-purpose LLM evaluators and the nuanced reasoning required for expert-level peer review. In contrast, our work trains a specialized generative reward model, CycleReviewer, specifically on peer review data to simulate multi-reviewer discussions and produce calibrated evaluations with higher consistency than individual human reviewers.

**Preference Optimization for Language Model Alignment.** Aligning language models with human preferences has been advanced substantially through preference-based optimization methods. Rafailov et al. [6] introduced Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which reparameterizes the RLHF objective to directly optimize a policy from preference pairs without training an explicit reward model, simplifying the alignment pipeline. Meng et al. [7] proposed SimPO, a reference-free alternative that uses the average log probability of a response as an implicit reward, eliminating the need for a reference model and reducing memory and computational overhead while aligning training more closely with generation metrics. Zhang et al. [8] explored generative reward modeling as next-token prediction, enabling LLMs to perform reasoning-based verification through chain-of-thought rationales. While these methods provide effective offline alignment, they are typically applied to general instruction-following or dialogue tasks. Our work extends SimPO to an iterative setting tailored for scientific research, where preference pairs are constructed from simulated peer reviews of generated papers, enabling the model to self-improve across successive research-review cycles.

**Detection of Machine-Generated Scientific Text.** As LLMs become increasingly capable of producing realistic scientific content, detecting machine-generated text has emerged as a critical safeguard for academic integrity. Mitchell et al. [9] introduced DetectGPT, a zero-shot detection method that leverages the negative curvature property of language model probability functions, where machine-generated text tends to lie in regions of negative log-probability curvature. Bao et al. [10] subsequently proposed Fast-DetectGPT, which achieves more efficient detection by using conditional probability curvature—comparing the probability of a passage under a scoring model against its probability after perturbation—without requiring multiple model calls for sampling. We adopt Fast-DetectGPT as part of our ethical safeguard framework to ensure that outputs generated by our models can be reliably distinguished from human-written papers, protecting the integrity of the research community.
