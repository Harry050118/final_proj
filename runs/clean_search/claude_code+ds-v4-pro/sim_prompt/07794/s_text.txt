**Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation.** Large language models (LLMs) increasingly act as agents that autonomously decide when and how to retrieve external knowledge. ReAct [1] pioneered the interleaving of reasoning traces with action steps, demonstrating that LLMs can synergistically combine chain-of-thought with tool use such as Wikipedia search. Toolformer [2] showed that LLMs can learn to decide when to call external APIs including search engines during generation. IRCoT [3] specifically interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning for knowledge-intensive multi-step questions, showing that each reasoning step can guide the next retrieval query. Self-RAG [4] introduced special reflection tokens that allow an LLM to decide on-the-fly whether to retrieve, and to critique its own generations, trained through supervised fine-tuning. FLARE [5] proposed active retrieval during generation by detecting when the model lacks confidence and issuing forward-looking retrieval queries. More recently, Search-o1 [6] extended agentic search to large reasoning models, integrating retrieval into the extended chain-of-thought reasoning process. These works establish the paradigm of agentic RAG but primarily rely on prompting or supervised fine-tuning, without explicit optimization of the search decision process itself.

**Reinforcement Learning for Agentic Search.** A growing body of work applies reinforcement learning (RL) to train LLMs for search-augmented reasoning. Search-R1 [7] proposed an RL framework where LLMs learn to interleave reasoning with search engine calls, using outcome-based rewards and format rewards computed over structured reasoning traces. R1-Searcher [8] introduced RL-based incentives for the search capability of LLMs, using retrieval-based outcome rewards to encourage effective use of search tools. R1-Searcher++ [9] extended this approach to incentivize dynamic knowledge acquisition, explicitly rewarding the model based on whether retrieved information contributes to correct answers. RAG-R1 [10] explored multi-query parallelism, training LLMs to generate multiple search queries simultaneously to improve retrieval coverage. DecEx-RAG [11] applied process supervision to agentic RAG, optimizing both the decision to search and the execution of search queries through a process-supervised RL pipeline. Beyond Outcome Reward [12] proposed decoupling the search and answering reward signals, showing that separate reward components for search quality and answer correctness improve agent performance. Despite these advances, existing methods rely on outcome-based or coarsely-grained reward signals that do not provide explicit step-level feedback on whether each individual search decision was necessary or optimal.

**Process Rewards and Fine-Grained Supervision for LLM Reasoning.** The idea of providing process-level rather than outcome-only rewards has gained traction in LLM training. Let's Reward Step by Step [13] introduced step-level process reward models (PRMs) for mathematical reasoning, demonstrating that fine-grained supervision at each reasoning step yields better alignment than final-answer-only feedback. DeepSeek-R1 [14] showed that large-scale RL with rule-based outcome rewards can elicit sophisticated reasoning behaviors, including self-verification and reflection, without explicit process supervision. DeepSeekMath [15] introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a critic-free RL algorithm that compares outputs within groups to compute relative advantages, later widely adopted for training reasoning and search agents. In the context of agentic RAG, however, process rewards remain underexplored: existing PRM methods focus on mathematical or code reasoning where step correctness is well-defined, while search trajectories present unique challenges including ambiguous step boundaries and the difficulty of determining whether a retrieval was truly necessary.

**Search Efficiency and Knowledge-Aware Retrieval.** A complementary line of work addresses the efficiency of retrieval-augmented systems by determining when retrieval is needed and when the model should rely on its parametric knowledge. When to Retrieve [16] proposed training LLMs to recognize questions that require retrieval versus those answerable from parametric knowledge alone. SeaKR [17] introduced self-aware knowledge retrieval, where the model assesses its own knowledge state to make adaptive retrieval decisions. KBM [18] delineated knowledge boundaries for LLMs to enable adaptive retrieval, training models to recognize the limits of their parametric knowledge. Several recent works specifically target suboptimal search behaviors in agentic systems. Search Wisely [19] proposed reducing over-search by modeling the model's uncertainty and suppressing searches when confidence is high. To Search or Not to Search [20] applied causal intervention to align the decision boundary of deep search agents, distinguishing necessary from unnecessary retrieval steps. AutoSearch [21] introduced adaptive search depth control via RL, learning to terminate search early when sufficient information has been gathered. Demystifying and Enhancing the Efficiency of LLM-Based Search Agents [22] conducted a systematic analysis of efficiency bottlenecks in search agents and proposed training strategies to reduce redundant retrievals. While these methods improve search efficiency through adaptive retrieval policies, confidence estimation, or coarse-grained penalties, none provides the fine-grained, step-specific feedback on individual retrieval decisions that is necessary to simultaneously suppress both over-search and under-search during training.
