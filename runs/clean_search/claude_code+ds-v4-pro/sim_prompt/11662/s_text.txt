**LLM-based Evaluation.** Large language models have been widely adopted as evaluators of text and model outputs. Zheng et al. [1] introduced the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, demonstrating that strong LLMs such as GPT-4 can approximate human preferences in open-ended dialogue evaluation, while also identifying key biases including position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases. Liu et al. [2] proposed G-Eval, a framework using GPT-4 with chain-of-thought prompting for NLG evaluation, achieving improved human alignment over prior reference-based and reference-free metrics. Subsequent work has systematically characterized biases that compromise LLM-based evaluation, including position bias [3], where models favor responses based on their placement in the prompt, and self-preference bias [4, 5], where LLM evaluators favor outputs generated by themselves or similar models. These biases persist despite careful prompt engineering and pose risks to the reliability of LLM-generated judgments. Our work extends the study of evaluation biases to the multimodal and interactive domain, identifying agreement bias---a tendency of MLLMs to over-validate agent behavior---and examining its consequences for downstream applications that rely on MLLM-based rewards.

**Verifiers and Reward Models.** Verifiers---functions assigning rewards aligned with desired criteria---have been instrumental to AI progress in domains with well-defined success metrics. In code and mathematics, process reward models (PRMs) that evaluate intermediate reasoning steps have been shown to outperform outcome-based supervision, enabling more reliable model training and test-time search [6]. The RLHF paradigm [7] established a framework for aligning language models with human preferences through learned reward models, inspiring subsequent work on self-rewarding approaches where language models generate their own training signals via LLM-as-a-Judge prompting [8]. Prover-verifier games [9] demonstrated that optimizing for verifiability can improve the legibility and correctness of model outputs. These lines of work highlight the central role of reliable reward signals, yet they have primarily focused on text-based or formally verifiable domains. Our work investigates the challenges of extending verification to open-ended, multimodal tasks that lack clear-cut success criteria.

**Multimodal Agents and Benchmarks.** The development of autonomous agents for digital and embodied environments has spurred the creation of realistic, multimodal benchmarks. WebArena [10] introduced a self-hosted web environment with functional websites spanning e-commerce, social forums, and development domains, evaluating text-based agents on long-horizon tasks. VisualWebArena [11] extended this paradigm with visually grounded tasks requiring image understanding, revealing that vision-language models outperform text-only agents but still achieve modest success rates. OSWorld [12] scaled agent evaluation to real computer environments with multi-application workflows across operating systems. In the robotics domain, benchmarks like robomimic [13] provide long-horizon manipulation tasks for learning from demonstrations. Agent architectures have evolved in parallel: ReAct [14] demonstrated the benefits of interleaving reasoning traces with actions; Set-of-Mark prompting [15] improved visual grounding in multimodal models; and several works have advanced GUI grounding for web and desktop agents through vision-based approaches [16, 17, 18]. The diffusion policy framework [19] enabled effective visuomotor policy learning for robotics tasks. Evaluation in these benchmarks predominantly relies on hand-crafted oracle scripts with privileged access to environment states, which are challenging to scale and maintain.

**MLLM-based Verification of Agent Behavior.** The use of multimodal models to evaluate agent trajectories is an emerging research area. Du et al. [20] proposed SuccessVQA, framing success detection as a visual question-answering task using vision-language models trained with human reward annotations. Pan et al. [21] developed domain-general neural evaluators for web navigation and device control trajectories, achieving strong agreement with oracle metrics and using these evaluators to improve agent performance. AgentRewardBench [22] introduced the first comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to evaluate LLM judges on web agent trajectory assessment, revealing that no single LLM consistently outperforms others across environments. Concurrently, Agent-RewardBench [23] proposed a step-level, multi-dimensional benchmark for reward modeling in multimodal agents across perception, planning, and safety dimensions. For GUI tasks, GUI-Shepherd [24] introduced a process reward model trained on human-annotated interaction data to provide dense, step-by-step feedback, while VAGEN [25] proposed agentic interactive verification that shifts from passive observation to proactive environment interaction. For robotic manipulation, AHA [26] developed a VLM-based framework for detecting and reasoning over failures. Our work complements these efforts by systematically characterizing a fundamental limitation---agreement bias---across a broader set of environments, model families, and applications, and by proposing Self-Grounded Verification (SGV), a lightweight method that leverages MLLMs' own sampling mechanisms to improve verification without additional training.

**Self-Improvement and Online Learning for Agents.** Verifier quality directly impacts methods that rely on automated evaluation to improve agent performance. Reflexion [27] introduced a verbal reinforcement learning paradigm where agents generate reflective summaries from environmental feedback, enabling self-correction without model fine-tuning. ReST meets ReAct [28] proposed a self-improvement framework combining reinforced self-training with ReAct-style reasoning for multi-step tasks. WebRL [29] demonstrated self-evolving online curriculum reinforcement learning for training web agents, where task generation and agent training co-evolve. These methods depend on reliable reward signals to filter trajectories and provide corrective feedback; when verifiers fail to identify flawed behavior, self-improvement stagnates. Our work examines how agreement bias impairs such downstream applications and shows that improved verification through SGV can restore and amplify the benefits of self-improvement and online supervision across digital and robotic domains.
