**LLMs for Research.** Recent years have seen growing interest in using language models to assist and automate various stages of scientific research. ResearchAgent [1] introduced an iterative framework that generates research ideas grounded in scientific literature, using entity-centric knowledge stores and multi-agent peer review to refine outputs. SciMON [2] optimized neural language models for generating novel scientific hypotheses from literature, moving beyond binary link prediction toward open-ended idea generation through in-context contrastive learning. For systematic literature synthesis, AutoSurvey [5] demonstrated that LLMs can automatically produce comprehensive surveys via retrieval-augmented generation and multi-LLM evaluation. MLAgentBench [3] established a benchmark for evaluating whether LLM-driven agents can autonomously design, execute, and iterate on machine learning experiments, covering the full experimental workflow from data preparation to result analysis. Most ambitiously, The AI Scientist [4] proposed a fully automated, prompt-driven pipeline capable of generating complete scientific papers end-to-end, integrating idea generation, experiment execution, and manuscript writing. However, these approaches predominantly rely on commercial, closed-source models and prompt-based orchestration, which limits their adaptability across the full research cycle and prevents them from being formulated as policy optimization problems amenable to reinforcement learning. Prompt-based methods also frequently struggle to produce research ideas that are both diverse and practically feasible, constraining their real-world applicability. In contrast, our work introduces an iterative preference training framework built entirely on open-source LLMs, enabling continuous self-improvement through a research-review-refinement cycle that addresses the limitations of static, prompt-driven pipelines.

**LLMs for Science Discovery.** The ambition to automate scientific discovery has deep historical roots, extending from early symbolic approaches to modern deep learning methods. Early work on automated scientific discovery focused on equation discovery and symbolic regression within constrained scientific domains [6]. The field has since progressed through successive stages — from rule-based expert systems to neural network-driven data analysis — with surveys documenting the evolution toward increasingly autonomous discovery systems [6, 7]. In the deep learning era, AI has been widely applied across domains such as chemistry, biology, and materials science, primarily as a passive tool for data analysis and pattern recognition within individual scientific fields. A persistent limitation identified in this trajectory is that AI has largely remained in a supporting role, assisting human scientists rather than actively driving new research directions [7]. The key challenge is enabling machines to move beyond analysis toward creative hypothesis formation and experimental design, which demands advanced reasoning capabilities and cross-domain knowledge synthesis. Our framework builds on this historical progression by training LLMs as autonomous agents capable of both proposing novel research directions and evaluating them through simulated peer review, shifting AI from a supporting instrument toward an active contributor in the scientific discovery process.

**Automated Evaluation of Research Papers.** The application of AI tools to scientific publishing and peer review has attracted considerable research attention. Liang et al. [8] conducted a large-scale empirical analysis of whether LLMs can provide useful feedback on research manuscripts, finding that LLM-generated critiques can overlap substantially with human expert comments while highlighting scalability challenges in conventional peer review. MARG [9] advanced this direction by proposing a multi-agent review generation approach in which multiple LLM instances engage in structured internal discussion, distributing paper text across agents to handle the full length of scientific manuscripts. Tyser et al. [10] systematically evaluated LLM-based academic review systems, benchmarking AI-generated reviews against human assessments and examining issues of scalability and bias awareness. On the evaluation methodology side, RewardBench [11] provided a systematic benchmark for assessing reward models in language modeling, revealing that purpose-trained reward models can exhibit different behavior from general-purpose LLM judges such as GPT-4. Generative Verifiers [12] further demonstrated that framing reward modeling as next-token prediction — rather than as a scalar regression — can enable more effective verification of complex reasoning outputs. Despite these advances, existing review approaches typically treat evaluation as a one-shot task without integrating reviewer feedback into an iterative improvement loop for the underlying research model. Our work differs by training a generative reward model (CycleReviewer) that simulates multiple reviewers with varying perspectives and generates structured feedback, which in turn provides reward signals for iterative policy optimization, enabling the research model to continuously refine its outputs based on automated peer evaluation.
