**LLM-as-Judge and MLLM-based Evaluation.** The use of large language models as evaluators has gained significant attention, with works demonstrating that LLMs can approximate human judgments across diverse tasks. Zheng et al. [1] introduced the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm with MT-Bench, showing that GPT-4 achieves over 80% agreement with human preferences while identifying biases such as position and verbosity effects. Liu et al. [2] extended this to NLG evaluation through G-Eval, using chain-of-thought prompting and token-level probability weighting to achieve better human alignment. These approaches have been adopted for evaluating agent behavior. AgentRewardBench [3] provides a comprehensive benchmark for assessing LLM judges on web agent trajectories, revealing that rule-based evaluation systematically underestimates agent success and that no single LLM consistently outperforms others across environments. Kwon et al. [4] explored using LLMs as proxy reward functions in reinforcement learning, demonstrating that LLM-generated rewards can enable data-efficient reward design without expert-crafted functions. These works motivate broader use of MLLMs as verifiers, but also surface concerns about bias and reliability that the present work investigates in depth.

**Web, Desktop, and Embodied Agent Benchmarks.** Evaluating MLLM verifiers requires diverse, realistic environments where verification demands nuanced multimodal reasoning. WebArena [5] introduced a self-hosted web environment with 812 long-horizon tasks across four functional domains, using functional correctness for evaluation. VisualWebArena [6] extended this to 910 visually grounded tasks requiring agents to process both text and image inputs. OSWorld [7] provides a scalable, real-world executable environment with 369 tasks spanning multi-application workflows on Ubuntu, Windows, and macOS, where state-of-the-art agents achieve only 12.24% success rate. For robotic manipulation, Mandlekar et al. [8] conducted a systematic study of offline learning from human demonstrations, introducing the robomimic framework with challenging long-horizon tasks such as tool-hang, emphasizing the importance of history-dependent models and dataset quality. These benchmarks collectively span web, desktop, and robotics domains, providing the foundation for studying MLLM-based verification in open-ended settings.

**Agent Self-Improvement with LLM Feedback.** Several works have explored using LLM-derived feedback to enable agents to self-improve. Shinn et al. [9] introduced Reflexion, a verbal reinforcement learning framework where language agents generate reflective summaries from environmental feedback and store them in episodic memory to guide future decisions, achieving significant gains in decision-making, reasoning, and programming tasks without model fine-tuning. Yuan et al. [10] proposed Self-Rewarding Language Models, where the model acts as its own judge via LLM-as-a-Judge prompting to generate preference data for iterative DPO training, demonstrating that both generation and reward quality improve across iterations. These approaches demonstrate the potential of LLM-derived feedback for agent improvement, but their effectiveness depends critically on the quality of the verifier's judgments—highlighting the importance of understanding and mitigating biases in MLLM-based evaluation.

**Biases in LLM-based Evaluation.** The reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge systems is compromised by systematic biases. Shi et al. [11] conducted a large-scale empirical study of position bias in LLM-as-a-Judge, identifying that bias is influenced by solution quality gaps and introducing metrics for position consistency and preference fairness. Spiliopoulou et al. [12] proposed a statistical framework to detect and quantify self-bias, finding that models systematically assign overly favorable ratings to their own outputs, and that family-bias further distorts cross-model evaluations. These biases have been shown to persist despite mitigation strategies such as label rephrasing and criteria-order randomization. The present work contributes to this line of inquiry by identifying a distinct form of bias—agreement bias—that manifests when MLLMs evaluate multimodal agent trajectories, and by proposing a method to mitigate it.
