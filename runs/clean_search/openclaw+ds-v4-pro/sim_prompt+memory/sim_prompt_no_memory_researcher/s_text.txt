**LLMs for Automated Scientific Research.** Recent years have seen growing interest in leveraging large language models to automate aspects of the research process. The AI Scientist [1] introduced a fully automated, prompt-driven pipeline capable of generating research papers end-to-end using commercial LLMs, demonstrating the feasibility of AI-driven scientific discovery. Similarly, AI-Researcher [2] proposed an autonomous framework that leverages LLM reasoning capabilities for scientific innovation across multiple domains. MLR-Copilot [3] focused on autonomous machine learning research using LLM-based agents, while the survey by Eger et al. [4] comprehensively reviewed the landscape of AI-assisted scientific discovery, experimentation, and content generation. On the idea generation front, Si et al. [5] conducted a large-scale human study demonstrating that LLMs can generate research ideas judged as more novel than those from human experts, though with limitations in feasibility. IdeaBench [6] provided a benchmark for systematically evaluating LLM-based research idea generation. For literature synthesis, AutoSurvey [7] showed that LLMs can automatically produce structured survey papers. Our work differs from these efforts in two key aspects: we train open-source LLMs rather than relying on commercial APIs, and we introduce an iterative self-improvement loop where a reward model provides feedback that directly optimizes the research policy.

**Preference Optimization for Language Models.** Our iterative training framework builds on advances in preference-based alignment. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [8] reformulated RLHF as a direct classification problem on preference pairs, eliminating the need for an explicit reward model. SimPO [9] further simplified this by introducing a length-normalized, reference-free reward formulation that reduces memory and computational overhead. Self-Rewarding Language Models [10] explored the idea of LLMs generating their own training signals through self-play, while iterative DPO methods [11] demonstrated that repeated rounds of sampling and optimization can yield consistent improvements over single-round approaches. Our Iterative SimPO framework extends these ideas to the research domain, where the CycleReviewer model acts as a specialized reward function that evaluates generated papers and produces preference pairs for policy optimization, with an added negative log-likelihood term to stabilize training on long-form scientific text.

**Automated Peer Review and Scientific Evaluation.** The application of AI to the peer review process has attracted substantial attention. Early work by Robertson [12] conducted a pilot study finding GPT-4 slightly helpful for peer review assistance, while the PeerRead dataset [13] enabled NLP research on review score prediction and decision analysis. More recently, the SEA framework [14] proposed standardized evaluation protocols for automated peer review systems. In the broader context of LLM-based evaluation, RewardBench [15] established that reward models specifically trained for evaluation tasks consistently outperform general-purpose LLMs acting as judges, motivating our approach of training a dedicated CycleReviewer model rather than relying on prompt-based evaluation. Our CycleReviewer extends these lines of work by simulating not just individual reviewers but an entire program committee process—including multiple reviewers with varying perspectives and a senior reviewer synthesizing final decisions—yielding both qualitative feedback and quantitative scores that serve as training signals.

**AI-Generated Text Detection and Ethical Safeguards.** As LLMs become increasingly capable of producing fluent scientific content, the need for reliable detection mechanisms grows. DetectGPT [16] proposed using probability curvature for zero-shot machine-generated text detection, and Fast-DetectGPT [17] improved upon this with a conditional probability curvature approach that achieves both higher accuracy and lower computational cost. We adopt Fast-DetectGPT as a practical safeguard in our framework, using it to classify whether generated papers are machine-produced, thereby protecting the integrity of the research community while pursuing automated scientific discovery.

**AI for Scientific Discovery.** The broader AI4Science movement has historically applied machine learning to accelerate domain-specific discovery in fields such as chemistry, biology, and materials science. Comprehensive surveys [4, 18] have documented this transition from passive data analysis tools toward systems that actively contribute to the scientific process. However, most prior work operates within single scientific domains and treats AI as an analytical tool rather than an autonomous research agent. Our work contributes to shifting this paradigm by enabling LLMs to actively drive the full research lifecycle—from literature review and hypothesis generation to manuscript preparation and iterative refinement—while maintaining ethical safeguards for academic integrity.
