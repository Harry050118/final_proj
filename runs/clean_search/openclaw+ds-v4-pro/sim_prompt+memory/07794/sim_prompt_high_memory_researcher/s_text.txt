**Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation.** Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has evolved from single-shot retrieval toward agentic frameworks where language models autonomously decide when and what to retrieve across multiple reasoning steps. Prompting-based approaches such as IRCoT [1] interleave chain-of-thought reasoning with retrieval, enabling LLMs to decompose complex questions and fetch relevant knowledge iteratively. Search-o1 [2] extends this paradigm with agentic search-enhanced reasoning, demonstrating that LLMs can effectively coordinate search tool usage through sophisticated prompting strategies. Building on the motivation that search behavior should be learned rather than scripted, reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied to train LLM search agents. Search-R1 [3] introduces an RL framework where LLMs learn to issue search queries and incorporate retrieved information through outcome-based rewards, demonstrating that language models can develop search and reasoning capabilities through RL training with outcome rewards. However, these methods typically rely on outcome-only rewards, which evaluate the final answer without providing feedback on the quality of individual search decisions, leaving the agent's search behavior largely unoptimized for efficiency.

**Efficient Search in Reinforcement Learning-Based Agents.** While agentic RAG enables more powerful reasoning, it introduces efficiency challenges: agents may over-search by retrieving redundant or unnecessary information, or under-search by relying on parametric knowledge when external retrieval is needed. Prior work has explored several strategies to address these inefficiencies. Length and retrieval-count penalties, as employed by β-GRPO [4], discourage unnecessarily long trajectories but risk over-suppressing legitimate search behavior when the model encounters knowledge gaps. R1-Searcher [5] and R1-Searcher++ [6] represent advances in RL-based search agent training, with the latter explicitly targeting search efficiency through improved training objectives. These methods improve overall search behavior but operate at the trajectory level, providing coarse-grained signals that cannot differentiate between individual optimal and suboptimal search steps within a reasoning sequence. Confidence-based and knowledge-awareness approaches have also been proposed to guide retrieval decisions, yet they rely on imperfect proxies that may misjudge the necessity of a search at a given reasoning step. Critically, none of these prior methods provides explicit, step-specific feedback on whether each retrieval decision was necessary or whether a missing search should have been conducted—a gap that demands fine-grained process-level supervision.

**Process-Level Supervision and Reinforcement Learning for Language Models.** Recent advances in RL for language model training have demonstrated the importance of reward design. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [7] has been widely applied to LLM fine-tuning, offering stable training dynamics through clipped policy updates. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [8] introduces a critic-free approach that leverages group-based advantage estimation, which has been applied to mathematical reasoning tasks. Beyond the choice of RL algorithm, the granularity of supervision has emerged as a critical design dimension. Process reward models that provide step-level verification signals have shown promise for improving reasoning quality in domains such as mathematical problem solving. However, applying process-level rewards to agentic RAG presents unique challenges: reasoning trajectories must be decomposed into discrete, evaluable steps, and the correctness of each search decision must be assessed against the agent's actual knowledge boundary rather than against a static reference. HiPRAG addresses these challenges by reformulating the agent's output into a structured, machine-parsable format that enables rule-based step decomposition, coupled with efficient, on-the-fly detection of over-search and under-search behaviors. Its hierarchical reward function gates the process-level bonus on format and answer correctness, ensuring that the agent first learns to reason with the search tool before being incentivized to optimize its search efficiency—thereby avoiding the over-suppression of retrieval capabilities that plagues simpler penalty-based approaches.
