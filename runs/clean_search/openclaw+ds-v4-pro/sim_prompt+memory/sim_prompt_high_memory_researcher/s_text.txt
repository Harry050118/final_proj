**LLMs for Research.** The use of large language models for automating research tasks has attracted growing attention. Early efforts explored multi-agent collaborative writing and multi-module retrieval to enhance research idea generation. ResearchAgent [1] introduced an iterative framework that grounds research ideas in scientific literature through entity-centric knowledge stores and multi-agent peer review. SciMON [2] optimized scientific idea generation for novelty by retrieving inspirations from existing literature and evaluating novelty relative to prior work. Si et al. [3] conducted a large-scale human study with over 100 NLP researchers to systematically evaluate whether LLMs can generate novel research ideas, providing rigorous human judgments on the quality of AI-generated proposals. Beyond idea generation, Wang et al. [4] demonstrated that LLMs can automatically compose structured survey papers by retrieving and synthesizing relevant literature. To evaluate LLMs' capabilities across the broader research process, Huang et al. [5] introduced MLAgentBench, a benchmark for assessing LLM agents on end-to-end machine learning experimentation tasks including data preparation, model implementation, and debugging. The AI Scientist [6] proposed an LLM-powered automated pipeline for open-ended scientific discovery that spans idea generation, code execution, experiment iteration, and manuscript writing. However, these approaches predominantly rely on static prompting of commercial LLMs, which constrains their ability to generate ideas that are both diverse and practically feasible, and they lack mechanisms for iterative improvement informed by structured quality feedback. To address this, we introduce an iterative self-rewarding framework that post-trains open-source LLMs through reinforcement learning with simulated peer review, enabling continuous refinement of research outputs.

**LLMs for Science Discovery.** The application of AI to scientific discovery has a long history, with early work applying AI techniques in fields such as chemistry, synthetic biology, material discovery, and mathematics. Recent surveys of AI4Science have documented the expanding role of neural networks and large language models across diverse scientific domains including molecular modeling, drug discovery, and physics simulation [7]. Complementary work has examined the capacity of LLMs for scientific synthesis, inference, and explanation across diverse research workflows [8]. However, AI in scientific discovery has primarily served as a passive tool — performing data analysis within individual domains without actively initiating or driving the discovery process. The key challenge is enabling AI to move beyond supporting analysis and actively contribute to generating and iteratively refining new research ideas, which demands advanced reasoning, creativity, and self-improvement capabilities. Our work builds on this trajectory by integrating the full research lifecycle, aiming to shift AI from a supporting tool to an active participant in the iterative cycle of ideation, manuscript preparation, peer review, and refinement.

**Automated Evaluation of Research Papers.** The use of AI tools in the scientific publishing process has gained considerable attention, encompassing applications such as summarizing paper content, detecting methodological issues, and identifying potential biases. Liang et al. [9] conducted a large-scale empirical analysis examining whether LLMs can provide useful feedback on research papers, finding that LLM-generated critiques exhibit overlap with human expert comments at rates comparable to inter-human reviewer agreement. Robertson [10] performed a pilot study assessing GPT-4's utility in peer-review assistance, providing early evidence on the strengths and limitations of general-purpose LLMs in this role. MARG [11] introduced a multi-agent framework for generating structured paper reviews, employing specialized agents to address different evaluation dimensions such as strengths, weaknesses, and overall assessment. Generative Verifiers [12] reformulated reward modeling as a next-token prediction task, demonstrating that generative reward models can serve as effective evaluators without requiring a separate scalar prediction head. However, when LLMs serve as judges of research quality, even advanced proprietary models remain limited compared to reward models specifically trained for evaluation tasks. RewardBench [13] provides a standardized benchmark for evaluating reward models, revealing systematic performance gaps between general-purpose LLM judges and purpose-trained reward models. This gap highlights the challenge of achieving human-level judgment and reasoning in AI-driven peer review. In contrast to these approaches, we train CycleReviewer as a generative reward model specifically on peer review data, simulating a comprehensive multi-reviewer evaluation process that captures diverse perspectives — spanning strengths, weaknesses, soundness, presentation, and contribution — before delivering a consolidated final assessment, which in turn provides the reward signal for iterative policy optimization of CycleResearcher.
