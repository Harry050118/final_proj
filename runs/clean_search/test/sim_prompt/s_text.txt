**Output Diversity and Mode Collapse in Language Models.** The tendency of language models to produce repetitive, low-diversity outputs has been recognized since early studies of neural text degeneration. Holtzman et al. [1] identified that maximization-based decoding strategies such as beam search cause language models to sample from the unreliable tail of the probability distribution, leading to bland and repetitive text, and proposed nucleus sampling as a remedy. Subsequent work has systematically characterized diversity collapse in the context of post-training and alignment. Mohammadi [2] showed that RLHF-aligned models exhibit lower token-level entropy, clustered embedding representations, and convergence toward attractor states, demonstrating that alignment reduces both syntactic and semantic diversity in creative generation tasks. Guo et al. [3] introduced a comprehensive framework for benchmarking linguistic diversity along lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions, finding that state-of-the-art LLMs systematically underrepresent creative linguistic structures compared to human text. Yun et al. [4] identified diversity collapse induced by structured prompt templates, showing that role markers and special tokens act as behavioral anchors that suppress output variation even under high-temperature sampling. Beyond diagnosing the problem, several works have proposed mitigation strategies. Nguyen et al. [6] introduced min-p sampling, a dynamic decoding strategy that adjusts the truncation threshold based on the top token's probability, improving both coherence and diversity at high temperatures. Most closely related to the target paper, Zhang et al. [5] proposed NoveltyBench, a benchmark for evaluating the ability of language models to generate multiple distinct and high-quality outputs. NoveltyBench uses curated prompts and filtered real-world queries and finds that larger models within a family often exhibit less diversity than smaller ones. However, existing diversity evaluations have largely focused on intra-model repetition, with limited attention to inter-model homogeneity, and have not provided a comprehensive taxonomy of the types of open-ended queries that users pose to language models in practice. This motivates the target paper's introduction of a large-scale dataset with a fine-grained taxonomy of real-world open-ended queries, as well as a systematic analysis of both intra-model repetition and cross-model output convergence.

**Real-World Conversation Datasets and Open-Ended Query Resources.** Large-scale datasets of real-world user interactions with language models have emerged as critical resources for studying LLM behavior in naturalistic settings. Zhao et al. [7] introduced WildChat, a corpus of one million user-ChatGPT conversations collected through an opt-in public chat service, encompassing over 2.5 million interaction turns with demographic metadata. WildChat has been shown to offer greater linguistic diversity and more varied use cases than prior instruction-tuning datasets. Zheng et al. [8] released LMSYS-Chat-1M, a dataset of one million real-world conversations with 25 different LLMs collected from the Vicuna demo and Chatbot Arena, demonstrating its utility for content moderation, safety benchmarking, and instruction-following training. These datasets provide valuable resources for understanding how users interact with LLMs at scale. However, they are primarily designed to capture broad interaction patterns rather than to systematically characterize open-ended queries that admit multiple valid responses. Neither dataset provides a systematic taxonomy of query types or distinguishes open-ended from closed-ended prompts. The target paper addresses this gap by filtering and categorizing open-ended user queries from WildChat, constructing the first comprehensive taxonomy of open-ended query types, and coupling this taxonomy with dense human annotations to enable the study of both generative diversity and pluralistic preference alignment.

**Pluralistic Alignment, Reward Modeling, and Human Preference Diversity.** Standard alignment pipelines typically assume a single, consensus notion of quality and train models to optimize for majority preferences. A growing body of work has challenged this assumption by examining annotator disagreement and modeling diverse human preferences. Davani et al. [9] showed that treating annotator judgments as separate subtasks rather than aggregating to a single ground truth preserves systematic individual differences and produces better-calibrated uncertainty estimates in subjective NLP tasks. Bakker et al. [10] fine-tuned a 70B-parameter LLM to generate consensus statements that maximize expected approval across groups with diverse opinions, demonstrating that language models can synthesize outputs reflecting heterogeneous preferences when trained with appropriate social welfare objectives. Castricato et al. [11] introduced PERSONA, a reproducible testbed for pluralistic alignment that procedurally generates diverse user profiles from demographic data and evaluates whether LMs can role-play users with varied values. Zhang et al. [12] developed a taxonomy of disagreement sources in human preference annotations, showing that over 30% of disagreements stem from genuine preference diversity rather than annotation error, and demonstrated that standard Bradley-Terry reward models fail to distinguish between consensus and contested preferences. Complementing these empirical findings, Xie et al. [13] provided a survey of personalized and pluralistic preference alignment techniques, categorizing methods into training-time, inference-time, and user-modeling approaches, and highlighting the lack of standardized benchmarks for evaluating personalization. In parallel, the evaluation of reward models themselves has been systematized through benchmarks such as RewardBench [14], which evaluates over 80 reward models across chat, reasoning, and safety domains, revealing systematic shortcomings in reasoning and generalization. However, existing work on pluralistic alignment has largely studied preference diversity through synthetic personas or aggregated crowd preferences, rather than through dense, per-example human annotations that capture the full distribution of individual annotator judgments on alternative responses to open-ended queries. The target paper contributes a large-scale dataset with 25 independent human annotations per example, enabling fine-grained analysis of when and how human preferences diverge, and systematically evaluates whether LMs, reward models, and LM judges remain calibrated to human ratings under conditions of high annotator disagreement and similar-quality alternative responses.
