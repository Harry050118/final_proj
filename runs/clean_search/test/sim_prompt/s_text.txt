**Mode Collapse and Output Diversity in Language Models.** Neural language models are known to suffer from text degeneration, where maximization-based decoding produces repetitive, dull, and sometimes inconsistent outputs [1]. This problem, often termed mode collapse, has spurred a growing body of work on evaluating and improving output diversity. Holtzman et al. [1] identified the likelihood trap and proposed nucleus sampling (top-p) to mitigate degenerate repetition. Su et al. [2] attributed degeneration to the anisotropic token representation space and introduced contrastive training (SimCTG) paired with contrastive search decoding to jointly improve coherence and diversity. More recently, several benchmarks have been proposed to systematically evaluate LM diversity. Zhang et al. [3] introduced NoveltyBench, a benchmark curated to elicit diverse answers from both filtered real-world queries and synthetic prompts, finding that larger models within a family often exhibit less diversity. Le Bronnec et al. [4] adapted precision and recall metrics from image generation to text, revealing a quality-diversity trade-off that intensifies after instruction tuning and RLHF. Guo et al. [5] proposed a comprehensive framework for benchmarking linguistic diversity across lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions, showing that current alignment pipelines reduce output diversity. These works collectively establish that diversity is a fundamental shortcoming of modern LMs, yet most evaluations rely on narrow or synthetic tasks rather than capturing the full range of open-ended queries encountered in real-world usage.

**Open-Ended Generation Benchmarks and Datasets.** Several recent efforts have sought to build resources for evaluating LMs on creative and open-ended tasks using real-world interactions. Zhao et al. [6] released WildChat, a corpus of one million user-ChatGPT conversations spanning diverse languages and use cases, providing a valuable source of authentic user prompts. Wu et al. [7] introduced WritingBench, a benchmark covering six core writing domains with 100 subdomains, paired with a query-dependent evaluation framework for criteria-aware scoring. However, these resources are not specifically designed to systematically characterize the full spectrum of open-ended prompts that admit multiple valid responses, nor do they provide the dense, distributional human annotations needed to study pluralistic preference alignment.

**Decoding Strategies for Output Diversity.** Beyond benchmark-driven evaluation, substantial effort has focused on improving output diversity at the decoding stage. Zhang et al. [8] formalized decoding as a multi-objective quality-diversity optimization problem and conducted the first large-scale comparison of decoding algorithms, finding that all methods perform similarly when diversity is the priority. More recently, Minh et al. [9] proposed min-p sampling, a dynamic truncation method that scales the sampling threshold by the top token's probability, achieving improved diversity without sacrificing coherence at higher temperatures. Despite these advances, diversity-oriented decoding strategies place the burden on users to select appropriate configurations and are not broadly adopted, motivating solutions at the training level that robustly preserve output diversity by default.

**Cross-Model Homogenization and AI Safety Risks.** In parallel, a growing literature has examined how LM usage and synthetic data propagation can reduce content diversity at scale. Padmakumar and He [10] showed that collaborative writing with instruction-tuned LMs reduces both lexical and content diversity across different human authors. Briesch et al. [11] demonstrated that self-consuming training loops—where LMs are trained on their own outputs—lead to declining output diversity over successive generations. Guo et al. [12] corroborated this finding, showing that recursive fine-tuning on synthetic text causes consistent decreases in lexical, syntactic, and semantic diversity, especially on creative tasks. Rios-Sialer [13] framed homogenization as a primary AI safety concern and proposed structure-aware diversity pursuit as a mitigation strategy. While these studies highlight the risks of homogenization, they primarily focus on single-model settings or human-AI co-writing, leaving the systematic study of inter-model output convergence across diverse LMs relatively underexplored.

**Human Preference Alignment and Reward Modeling.** Aligning LMs with human preferences has become a central paradigm, driven by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) [14] and direct preference optimization (DPO) [15]. These methods rely on reward models trained on human preference judgments, and their evaluation has been advanced by benchmarks such as RewardBench [16]. High-quality preference datasets such as HelpSteer2 [17] and HelpSteer3 [18] provide multi-attribute human annotations for training reward models, though they typically contain sparse annotations (e.g., 2–3 annotators per example). For scalable evaluation, LLM-based judges have emerged as a popular alternative: Prometheus [19] trains an open-source evaluator LM using fine-grained score rubrics, while Zheng et al. [20] introduced MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena to validate LLM-as-a-judge against human preferences. These pipelines, however, are predominantly designed and evaluated under the assumption that a single consensus notion of quality exists, potentially overlooking the diverse, idiosyncratic preferences that characterize open-ended user queries.

**Pluralistic Preferences and Annotator Disagreement.** Recognizing that human preferences are inherently heterogeneous, a nascent line of work has begun to move beyond consensus-based alignment. Bakker et al. [21] fine-tuned LMs to generate statements that maximize expected approval across groups with diverse opinions, demonstrating the feasibility of modeling population-level preference distributions. Padmakumar et al. [22] identified two key dimensions of subjectivity—plurality of valid responses and indistinguishability of paraphrases—and showed that standard reward models correlate weakly with aggregate user preferences in these cases. Zhang et al. [23] developed a taxonomy of disagreement sources in human preference annotations and found that standard reward models and LLM judges consistently produce decisive judgments even on examples with substantial annotator divergence. A recent survey by Xie et al. [24] provides a comprehensive overview of personalized and pluralistic preference alignment techniques. However, existing work in this direction is limited by the scarcity of datasets with dense, multi-annotator labels on open-ended queries, which are necessary to rigorously study the relationship between annotator disagreement, response diversity, and model calibration.
