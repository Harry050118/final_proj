## Related Work

**LM Output Diversity and Mode Collapse.**
The tendency of language models to produce repetitive or degenerate text has been recognized since early work on neural text generation. Holtzman et al. [1] identified that maximization-based decoding leads to repetitive, incoherent outputs and proposed nucleus sampling to preserve diversity. Subsequent work has introduced decoding-level interventions such as min-p sampling [2], which dynamically adjusts the truncation threshold based on model confidence to improve output diversity at higher temperatures. Beyond decoding, several benchmarks now systematically evaluate LM generation diversity. NoveltyBench [3] evaluates the ability of LMs to produce multiple distinct, high-quality outputs across subjective, creative, and knowledge-based prompts, revealing that larger models within the same family often exhibit less diversity than their smaller counterparts. Guo et al. [4] proposed a framework for benchmarking linguistic diversity across lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions, finding that state-of-the-art LLMs significantly underperform human text in syntactic and semantic variation, particularly after preference tuning. Yun et al. [5] identified diversity collapse induced by structured prompt templates, showing that format tokens act as behavioral anchors that constrain the output space even under high-temperature sampling. At a broader level, Briesch et al. [6] demonstrated that training LLMs in a self-consuming loop on their own outputs progressively reduces generation diversity. While these works establish important foundations, existing benchmarks predominantly target stylized or narrow tasks and do not capture the full spectrum of real-world open-ended user queries. Moreover, prior studies have largely focused on intra-model repetition; the phenomenon of inter-model homogeneity—where distinct models independently converge on similar outputs—remains underexplored. This motivates the target paper's systematic study of both intra- and inter-model mode collapse across a broad set of models on real-world open-ended prompts.

**Real-World Open-Ended Query Resources.**
Understanding the types of open-ended queries users pose to LMs requires datasets grounded in authentic interactions. WildChat [7] provides a corpus of one million real-world user-ChatGPT conversations, capturing diverse languages, interaction patterns, and use cases far richer than curated instruction-tuning datasets. Wang et al. [8] developed a taxonomy of user intents in LLM interactions based on real-world logs, identifying categories such as creative generation, learning, and decision support. However, neither resource provides a systematic taxonomy of open-ended query types specifically designed to elicit diverse responses, nor do they couple query categorization with model diversity evaluation. This motivates the target paper's construction of INFINITY-CHAT, which combines a comprehensive open-ended query taxonomy with large-scale diversity analysis and dense human annotations.

**Automated Evaluation and Pluralistic Alignment for Open-Ended Tasks.**
Evaluating open-ended LM outputs requires handling subjective and often divergent human preferences. Zhang et al. [9] analyzed sources of annotator disagreement in preference datasets, finding that over 30% of disagreements stem from genuine preference divergence rather than annotation noise, and showed that standard Bradley-Terry reward models fail to distinguish between unanimous and divided preferences. To address this, pluralistic alignment frameworks have been proposed. Chen et al. [10] introduced PAL, which models heterogeneous human preferences as a mixture of latent prototypes, enabling reward functions that generalize to unseen users. Halpern et al. [12] proposed pairwise-calibrated reward ensembles that directly match the distribution of annotator preferences without requiring annotator identity labels. On the evaluation side, the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm [11] has emerged as a scalable alternative to human evaluation for open-ended tasks, but its reliability under high annotator disagreement and for responses of comparable quality remains poorly understood. These lines of work highlight the need for dense human annotations that capture distributional preferences, as well as reward models and judges that are calibrated to the full spectrum of human judgments on open-ended queries—gaps that the target paper addresses through its large-scale annotation study.
